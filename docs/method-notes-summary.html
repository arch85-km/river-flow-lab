Method Notes in Brief

The short version: what the tool computes, and — the part that matters most when you cite it — what it does not model at all. It solves one steady water surface along a river, section by section; it has no time in it and no second dimension, and those two absences shape every limitation below. The full method notes give the equations, the measurements and the references behind every statement here.

Audience Architecture and urban design students and staff Engine Steady 1D standard step · Manning Runs Entirely in the browser Version 0.1.0 · in development

Four things to know before you cite a number

  1. It is a teaching model, not a flood risk assessment. A template or drawn river has idealised levels, and a channel carved into a terrain model is an estimate of a bed the model does not record.
  2. The flow is steady. The peak you set is held for ever, with no storage and no timing, so a short flood would not reach the levels shown.
  3. It is one-dimensional. It cannot follow water round the end of a wall or across a floodplain, and buildings report a depth but do not block the flow.
  4. Results near a bridge depend on section spacing. On a test reach the afflux read 262 mm with sections 100 m apart and 195 mm from 10 m. Compare designs on the same sections and read the change.

What it calculates

One water surface along a reach for one steady flow, and the same reach without your design, so every result reads as a change. From the water surface come the figures below, section by section.

The water surface is found by the standard-step method: from the downstream end upwards, each section's level balances the energy equation with friction and with contraction and expansion losses. Each section is split at its banks into channel and floodplains with their own roughness, so a shallow floodplain carries little flow beside a deep channel.

The three flow buttons are anchors, not return periods. River full is the flow that brings the river to its lower bank top at the chosen section; Normal day is a quarter of it and Major flood twice it. The tool has no hydrology: a design flow has to come from elsewhere.

Not modelled at all

The big one
Time
No hydrograph, no storage on the floodplain, no travel time. The peak is held for ever, which overstates the levels a short, sharp flood would reach.
Out
A second dimension
Water moves along the river only. Flow round the end of a flood wall is judged by a rule — a wet floodplain just past an open end counts as reaching the building behind — not calculated.
Out
Supercritical flow
Where the flow is squeezed too hard, the section is held at critical depth and flagged. The flag marks where a fuller model would be needed.
Out
Culverts, debris, rain on the site
River flooding through open channels and bridges only. A blocked bridge opening can be imitated with an obstruction.
Partly
Sediment
With the bed's material chosen, it says where the water can move the bed, where material builds up and where the bed is stable, by Shields' threshold. How deep it wears away and how the bed changes over time are not modelled.
Partly
Buildings and eddies
Buildings report the depth over their floor but do not block the flow; add an obstruction to make one an obstacle. Eddy zones behind a blockage are sized by ratios and carry no flow below its top; the swirl inside them is not computed.
Partly
The river bed in a terrain model
Most terrain models record the water surface. Unless told otherwise, the tool carves a channel of the depth and width you give and labels the reach as estimated.

How exact the numbers are

0.03 mm
Backwater curve
The worst difference over 1 km, with sections 20 m apart, from an independent RK4 integration of the gradually varied flow equation. Normal and critical depth match their closed forms to four decimals.
+5%
Section spacing at a bridge
The afflux on a test reach was 204 mm at the template's 15 m spacing against 195 mm once settled at 10 m, and 262 mm at 100 m. Closer sections near a structure give a steadier figure.
1.44
Weir over a bridge deck
When water goes over a deck, the flow divides between the opening and a broad-crested weir with HEC-RAS's coefficient for decks, drowned by a high level below. The head is the depth over the deck, without the approach velocity head, which errs towards a higher level. It matches a hand summation to 0.1 m³/s.
check
Yarnell's pier equation
Reported beside the energy method at each bridge with piers, as a second opinion on the rise the piers alone cause. It matches a hand calculation exactly.
m AOD
Terrain positions
A GeoTIFF in British National Grid keeps its real position, good to a few metres; one in another grid is not placed on the map. An ASC file cannot say, so the grid is assumed and the window says so.

These figures are internal consistency, not validation. They come from the tool's own checks against closed-form results and independent calculations. None is a comparison with measured river levels or with another model's run.

Against HEC-RAS

Provenance. A comparison of methods as the code states them. No run of HEC-RAS on the same reach stands behind it.

The engine follows HEC-RAS's steady-flow method: the standard step, conveyance subdivision, blocked obstructions, levees and ineffective areas, the energy method through a bridge with Yarnell's equation alongside, and a weir over an overtopped deck. It differs in making eddy zones automatically from fixed ratios and clearing them gradually, in leaving the approach velocity out of the weir head, in drowning the deck weir by Villemonte's equation rather than HEC-RAS's curve, in offering the energy method only at bridges, and in having no supercritical or mixed-flow regime.

Where it stops

The tool is for teaching relationships and orders of magnitude — why a bridge opening raises the water upstream and not downstream, what a flood wall does to the next street, how a floodplain changes a river's response to more flow. For a flood risk assessment, a planning submission or any figure someone will rely on, use a validated model run by someone qualified to run it. A steady, one-dimensional model is too simple to serve that purpose.
