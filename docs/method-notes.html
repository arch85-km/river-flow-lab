River Flood Lab — Method Notes

What this tool computes, how it computes it, and — the part that matters most when you cite it — what it does not model at all. Everything below is read from the source code and the tool's own checks, not from what a river model is generally assumed to do.

Audience Architecture and urban design students and staff Engine Steady 1D standard step · Manning Runs Entirely in the browser Version 0.1.0 · in development

Four things to know before you cite a number

  1. It is a teaching model, not a flood risk assessment. The template reach and a river drawn on the map have idealised levels, not a survey, and a channel carved into a terrain model is an estimate of a bed the model does not record. Every page and export says so.
  2. The flow is steady. The peak flow you set is held for ever: there is no hydrograph, no storage on the floodplain and no timing. A short, sharp flood would not reach the levels shown; a long one could.
  3. It is one-dimensional. Water moves along the river only. It cannot follow a flood round the end of a wall or across a floodplain, so the tool judges those cases with simple rules and says when it has. Buildings report the depth over their floor but do not block the flow.
  4. Results near a bridge depend on how far apart the sections are. On a test reach the afflux read 255 mm with sections 100 m apart and settled at 188 mm from 10 m. Compare designs on the same sections, read the change rather than the level, and state the spacing.
01

What it calculates

One water surface along a reach of river for one steady flow, then the same reach without your design, so that every result can be read as a change. From the water surface it derives the figures below, section by section.

 The CSV carries every row above to three decimals, with the settings in its header.

The three flow buttons are set from the river itself. River full is the flow at which the river without your design reaches the lower of its two bank tops at the chosen section, found by bisection. Normal day is a quarter of that and Major flood twice it. These are teaching anchors, not return periods: the tool has no hydrology, and a 1-in-100-year flow has to come from somewhere else. A climate allowance in per cent multiplies whatever flow is set.

A flow from a float survey

A flow measured on site can be entered from a simple survey: the channel's width and mean depth, and the time a float takes over a measured distance. The surface runs faster than the water near the bed, so the mean speed is taken as a fraction of the float's, 0.85 unless changed (0.8 to 0.9 is usual). The climate allowance is set to 0, as a survey measures the flow on the day.

V_float = distance / time
V_mean  = 0.85 · V_float
Q       = V_mean · width · mean depth

The tool then compares its own channel speed at the chosen section with the survey's mean speed. Where the river's width and depth match the site, a large difference points to the roughness: Manning's n can be adjusted until the two agree, a simple calibration.

02

The river as cross-sections

The river is a row of cross-sections. Each is a ground line of offset and level pairs, read left to right looking downstream, with two bank positions and three Manning's n values. Chainage is measured upstream from the downstream end. Where a design element or an eddy zone needs a section the survey does not have, one is interpolated between its neighbours, point by point on the union of their fractional offsets.

At a trial water level each section is split at its banks into the left overbank, the channel and the right overbank, and each part carries its own conveyance:

K = (1/n) · A · R^(2/3),    R = A / P                          per part
α = (ΣA)² · Σ(Kᵢ³ / Aᵢ²) / (ΣK)³                                velocity-head coefficient

Splitting the section this way is what lets a shallow, rough floodplain carry little flow beside a deep channel. Without it a section that spills onto a floodplain would gain a large area and look faster than it is.

Water that cannot join the flow

  • Low ground behind a natural ridge carries no flow, and is shown dry, until the water rises over the ground between. The test is made on the natural ground, so a design element does not cut off water that could pass it.
  • Behind a flood wall, the ground carries no flow until the water overtops the crest; then all of it does.
  • Water above the end of a ground line is held by a vertical wall, flagged in the table, which adds to the wetted perimeter. If this appears, the section is too narrow for the flood.
03

The water profile

The profile is solved by the standard-step method, from the downstream end upwards, one section at a time. At each step the level upstream is found that balances the energy equation:

WS₂ + α₂V₂²/2g  =  WS₁ + α₁V₁²/2g + hₑ
hₑ = L · S̄f + C · | α₂V₂²/2g − α₁V₁²/2g |
S̄f = ( 2Q / (K₁ + K₂) )²                                    average conveyance
L  = (L_lob·Q̄_lob + L_ch·Q̄_ch + L_rob·Q̄_rob) / Q̄            discharge-weighted

The reach lengths differ for the two overbanks on a bend, worked out from the line on the map. C is a contraction coefficient where the flow speeds up and an expansion coefficient where it slows: 0.1 and 0.3, or 0.3 and 0.5 between a bridge and its neighbouring sections — the values HEC-RAS recommends for gradual transitions and for typical bridges. They are not shown in the interface.

The downstream end

The level at the downstream end is normal depth on the river bed's own fall there: 2 in 1,000 on the template, and the fall of the shape or the fitted bed on a drawn or terrain reach. It is set with the reach and not shown in the interface, to keep it simple for students; a project file can still carry a fixed level. Either way it is never below critical depth: if it would be, the tool uses critical depth and flags the section. Its influence fades upstream, and the runs with and without a design share it, so the change a design makes depends on it far less than the levels themselves. Keep a design well upstream of the downstream end.

Critical depth and choking

Critical depth is the level of least specific energy, found by scanning 400 levels from the bed to 20 m above the highest ground and refining the best one. Where no subcritical level upstream can satisfy the energy equation — the flow is squeezed harder than the section can pass at the energy available — the section is set to critical depth and flagged, and the status says The flow is squeezed. The model does not compute supercritical flow; a flagged section marks where a fuller model would be needed.

04

Obstructions and eddy zones

An obstruction is a blocked area: over its length along the river, the ground under it is raised to its top. The section loses that area and gains its sides as wetted perimeter. An imported 3D model is treated the same way, as solid from the ground up to its top, cut along the flow into strips.

Beside and behind a blockage the flow separates, leaving eddy zones of dead water. The tool sizes them from the expansion and contraction ratios of the HEC-RAS bridge method:

downstream length = ER · w      blockage against a side;  ER · w / 2 in mid-stream
upstream length   = CR · w      CR · w / 2 in mid-stream
ER = 2,  CR = 1                  this tool's defaults; both can be changed

HEC-RAS gives no single value for either ratio. It reports expansion ratios from about 0.8 to 3.6, depending on the opening and the flow, and treats a contraction ratio of 1 as a rule of thumb. ER 2 and CR 1 are this tool's choices within those ranges, not HEC-RAS defaults.

Within a zone the dead water narrows in a straight line as the flow re-expands. Below the top of the blockage the zone carries no flow. Above it, the dead water stays still and only the layer over it flows, as its own part of the section, so the zone clears gradually as the water rises. The swirl inside the zone is not computed. A zone shorter than the section spacing is given a section of its own, so it is never missed. Strips of an imported model make no eddy zones.

05

Bridges

A bridge is an opening between two abutments, with piers inside it, under a deck with a soffit (its underside) and a top. Abutments and piers block the section up to the deck top. An abutment runs to the valley side unless given a length; past a short abutment the flood passes round the bridge.

Through the opening

The flow through the opening is found by the same energy equation, with the bridge's own loss coefficients. When the water reaches the soffit, the soffit closes the section (pressure flow): water above it is cut off and the soffit becomes wetted perimeter. The soffit acts only where the deck is.

Over the deck

When the water at the section above the bridge is higher than the deck, the flow divides between the opening and the deck, which acts as a broad-crested weir. The division is found by bisection until both routes give the same level upstream:

Q_weir = Σ  C · H^1.5 · Δy · (1 − (h_d / H)^1.5)^0.385         summed in strips across the deck
C   = 1.44 m^0.5/s                                              HEC-RAS, for bridge decks
H   = upstream level − max(deck top, ground)                    depth over the deck
h_d = downstream level − max(deck top, ground)                  drowning, Villemonte 1947

The head is the depth of water over the deck, without the approach velocity head. That keeps the level from jumping as overtopping starts, and errs towards a higher level. A high water level below the bridge drowns the weir and passes less over it, by Villemonte's equation; HEC-RAS uses a different curve here (section 11).

What is reported

  • Afflux: the biggest rise above the same river with no bridges, from the bridge's upstream face to the next bridge upstream. The section just above a bridge can sit in the drawdown as the flow speeds into the opening, so it is not read alone.
  • Soffit clearance: the soffit level less the highest water under the bridge.
  • Flow regime: open, pressure flow, or overtopping, with how much goes over the deck.
  • Yarnell's pier rise, as a check on the energy method, with the pier shape factor K = 1.25 (square nose), 0.9 (semicircular) or 1.05 (triangular, 90°), as HEC-RAS tabulates them:
Δy = 2K · (K + 10ω − 0.6) · (a + 15a⁴) · V²/2g,    ω = V²/(2g·y)
V and y downstream of the bridge;  a = the share of the open area the piers take up
06

Flood walls, buildings and floating objects

A flood wall runs along one bank between two chainages. The ground behind it carries no flow until the water overtops its crest. Where a wall is too short to reach a section, it is given one.

A building reports the water level at its own chainage and the depth over its floor. It is dry if it stands on low ground cut off by a natural ridge, or behind a wall that holds at its own chainage on its own side — unless the floodplain just past an open end of the wall is wet, in which case water is taken to come round the end and the status says so. A wall tied into high ground still protects. This is a rule, not a calculation: a 1D model cannot follow water along the back of a wall. Buildings do not block the flow; to make one an obstacle, add an obstruction or import its model.

A floating object is a moored box. Only the part below the water blocks the flow: the water above its underside is removed, its underside and sides become wetted perimeter, and flow passes beneath it. It rises and falls with the water but cannot drift, because the model is steady. A float deeper than the water blocks down to the bed. Its depth below the water can be given directly or as a weight: a floating box sinks until it pushes aside its own weight of water, so the depth is the weight divided by 1,000 kg/m³ and by its length and width in plan. Its height above the water changes nothing in the results.

The underside acts like an ice cover: it adds friction to the flow beneath it. A hull is far smoother than a river bed, so its wetted perimeter carries its own roughness, Manning's n 0.013 unless changed in Advanced, combined with the bed's by Horton's rule as HEC-RAS combines roughness across a channel:

n = ( Σ Pᵢ · nᵢ^1.5  /  Σ Pᵢ )^(2/3)          Pᵢ the wetted perimeter of the bed and of the hull

So a float only millimetres deep changes the water very little, and a heavier one raises it mainly by the flow area it blocks.

Building from a terrain model

Most terrain models, including LIDAR, record the water surface rather than the river bed. So by default the tool carves a channel of the depth, bed width and bank slope given, and labels the reach as estimated. The bank tops are read from the model and smoothed over seven sections, so one rough cell does not set the depth. The bed is fitted so it never rises going downstream and stays at least 0.3 m below the lower bank. Site measurements of width and depth, by distance from the upstream end, override the sliders. The roughness of a terrain reach is the template's, 0.035 in the channel and 0.06 on the floodplains.

The ground each side is read out to the width asked for, across gaps up to 10 m wide where the model has no data, as where LIDAR masks out the water. If the model is said to hold the real bed but has no levels across the river, the tool says so rather than drawing a straight line over the gap.

Positions and distances

A GeoTIFF is read for the grid it declares. In British National Grid it keeps its real position and its levels are labelled m AOD; in any other grid, or with coordinates outside Great Britain, it is not placed on the map. An ASC file cannot say which grid it uses, so British National Grid is assumed and the window says so. The conversion to latitude and longitude uses the Ordnance Survey formulae, good to a few metres — enough to mark a place, not to set out a site. Distances on the map use the length of a degree on the WGS84 ellipsoid at the river's latitude.

The spacing of sections

A bridge or a blockage acts on the sections that cross it, and its losses are spread over the reach lengths either side. So the result depends on how close the sections are. Measured on a 1 km test reach with a bridge, at 150 m³/s:

Section spacingAffluxRise 300 m upstream
100 m255 mm244 mm
50 m237 mm227 mm
25 m217 mm207 mm
15 m (the template)197 mm188 mm
10 m188 mm179 mm

 The figure settles from about 10 m. At the template's 15 m it is about 5 per cent high.

Steadiness

As the flow rises the level upstream of a bridge rises with it, without jumps, through pressure flow and into overtopping: by at most 0.11 m for each 10 m³/s with eddy zones off, and 0.20 m for each 20 m³/s with them on, on the test reach.

These figures are internal consistency, not validation. They show the engine solves the equations it states, correctly and steadily. None is a comparison with measured river levels or with a run of another model, and they should not be described as validation.

11

Compared with HEC-RAS

Provenance. This section compares the methods as the code states them with HEC-RAS's published method. No run of HEC-RAS on the same reach stands behind it.

The engine follows HEC-RAS's steady-flow method closely: the standard step, conveyance subdivision at the banks, average-conveyance friction slope, discharge-weighted reach lengths, contraction and expansion losses, blocked obstructions, ineffective flow areas and levees that carry no flow until overtopped, the energy method through a bridge with Yarnell's equation alongside, and a weir over an overtopped deck with the coefficient HEC-RAS gives for decks. Where it differs:

  • Eddy zones are made from the contraction and expansion ratios automatically, and clear gradually above the blockage, where HEC-RAS asks the modeller to place ineffective areas and switches them on or off at a level.
  • The ratios for eddy zones are fixed defaults, ER 2 and CR 1, where HEC-RAS gives ranges and regression equations for the modeller to choose from.
  • The weir head leaves out the approach velocity head.
  • A drowned deck passes less flow by Villemonte's equation. HEC-RAS uses the submergence curve for trapezoidal weirs from Hydraulics of Bridge Waterways (Bradley 1978), and by default passes to the energy method above 95 per cent submergence.
  • Bridges use the energy method only. HEC-RAS also offers the momentum method, WSPRO, and orifice equations for pressure flow.
  • No mixed-flow regime: supercritical flow is not computed.

For a flood risk assessment, a planning submission or any figure someone will rely on, use a validated model run by someone qualified to run it.
